How China Works

A plain-language guide to China's political system, institutions, and decision-making — written for Canadian audiences

How China Actually Works

China is governed by a single-party system in which the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) sits above and directs the entire state apparatus. There is no separation of powers in the Western sense — no independent judiciary, no opposition parties with real power, and no legislature that operates as a genuine check on executive authority. Instead, the party leads everything: the government, the military, the courts, the media, and the major state-owned enterprises that drive the economy.

This is fundamentally different from how Canada and other Western democracies work. In Canada, the Prime Minister leads the government but is constrained by Parliament, the courts, and an independent press. In China, the General Secretary of the CCP leads the party, and the party leads the country. Government ministries, provincial leaders, and military commanders all answer ultimately to party structures, not to the public through elections.

Understanding this dual structure — party sitting above and interpenetrating the state — is the single most important thing for any Canadian trying to make sense of China's behavior on the world stage. Without this foundation, it is impossible to accurately interpret diplomatic signals, trade decisions, military postures, or the actions of Chinese state-linked companies operating in Canada.

Why it matters for Canada

Canada's diplomatic, trade, and security interactions with China are shaped by this party-state structure at every level. When Canadian officials negotiate with Chinese counterparts, they are engaging with people who operate within a chain of command that runs upward to the party, not to voters. Knowing who actually makes decisions — and through what process — helps Canadian policymakers, journalists, and citizens interpret signals correctly and avoid costly misreadings of Chinese intentions.

The Communist Party (CCP)

The Chinese Communist Party is the most powerful institution in China. Founded in 1921 and in power since 1949, the CCP has approximately 98 million members — making it the largest political party in the world. But membership does not equal influence. Power is concentrated at the very top, in a pyramid structure that narrows dramatically at each level.

At the apex sits the General Secretary, currently Xi Jinping, who has held the position since 2012. The General Secretary is the most powerful person in China — this title matters more than the presidency, which is largely ceremonial. Xi also holds the titles of President and Chairman of the Central Military Commission, consolidating all three pillars of power in one person. This level of concentration has not been seen since Mao Zedong.

Below the General Secretary is the Politburo Standing Committee (PSC), a body of seven men who collectively form the supreme decision-making authority in China. They meet regularly, their deliberations are secret, and their decisions are final. Below them is the full Politburo of 24 members, which includes the PSC plus additional senior leaders. The Central Committee of roughly 200 full members and 170 alternates is the next tier — it includes provincial leaders, military chiefs, and heads of major state institutions. At the base is the National Party Congress, roughly 2,300 delegates who convene every five years to ratify leadership changes and amend the party constitution.

The party's internal hierarchy is the real power map of China. Provincial governors, military commanders, and ministers all hold their positions at the party's pleasure. Promotion depends on party loyalty, factional connections, and — under Xi — demonstrated personal allegiance to the top leader. The party also controls all major personnel appointments through its Organization Department, which manages the careers of roughly 70 million cadres across the system.

Why it matters for Canada

The PSC's composition after each Party Congress signals China's policy direction for the next five years. When Xi stacked the 2022 PSC entirely with loyalists, it told the world that his priorities — self-reliance, national security, assertive foreign policy — would intensify, not moderate. Personnel IS policy in China. Canadian analysts who track leadership changes can anticipate shifts in China's approach to trade, technology, and diplomacy well before formal announcements are made.

The Government (State Council)

The State Council is China's cabinet — the executive arm of the government responsible for turning party decisions into concrete policy and administrative action. It is led by the Premier (currently Li Qiang), who is technically the head of government and manages the day-to-day running of the country's massive bureaucracy.

The State Council oversees China's ministries and commissions, which handle the functional work of governance. The most important ones for understanding China's external behavior include:

  • National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC / 发改委) — China's powerful economic planning body. It sets industrial policy, approves major projects, and manages price controls. It shapes the structural forces behind China's economic engagement with the world.
  • Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM / 商务部) — Handles trade policy, tariffs, foreign investment approvals, and trade disputes. This is the ministry Canadians most often encounter in the trade arena.
  • Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MFA / 外交部) — China's diplomatic arm. Its spokespersons deliver official positions, but the MFA does not set foreign policy — it communicates decisions made elsewhere.
  • Ministry of Finance (MOF / 财政部) — Manages government revenue, fiscal policy, and sovereign debt. Important for understanding China's economic health and spending priorities.

A critical point for Canadian observers: the State Council implements but does not set national direction. Policy direction comes from the party — from the PSC, from Leading Small Groups chaired by Xi Jinping, and from the Central Committee's plenary sessions. The Premier and ministers execute these directives. Under Xi, the State Council's autonomy has diminished significantly compared to earlier eras. The Premier today operates with considerably less latitude than predecessors like Zhu Rongji or Wen Jiabao enjoyed.

Why it matters for Canada

When MOFCOM imposes tariffs on Canadian canola or launches an anti-dumping investigation into Canadian lumber, it is executing a decision that likely originated at the party level. The ministry is the messenger, not the decision-maker. This means that resolving trade disputes with China requires understanding where in the party hierarchy the relevant decision was made — and at what level it can be reversed. Talking only to MOFCOM officials may not be enough if the directive came from much higher up.

The Legislature: NPC and CPPCC

China has a legislature, but it does not function like the Canadian Parliament, the U.S. Congress, or the British House of Commons. The National People's Congress (NPC) is constitutionally described as "the highest organ of state power," but in practice it serves to ratify and legitimize decisions already made by the party.

The full NPC comprises approximately 3,000 delegates who meet once a year in March for about two weeks — a period known as the "Two Sessions" (because the CPPCC meets concurrently). During this session, delegates review and approve the government work report, the national budget, and major legislation. Votes are overwhelmingly in favour; dissent is rare and carefully managed. The NPC has never rejected a major piece of legislation proposed by the party leadership.

Between sessions, the NPC Standing Committee (about 175 members) handles legislative business, interprets the constitution, and passes laws. This body has more substantive influence than the full NPC, though it too operates within parameters set by the party. In recent years, the NPC Standing Committee has been the vehicle for significant legislation including Hong Kong's national security law and revisions to China's espionage and data security laws — all of which have direct implications for Canadians operating in or with China.

The Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference (CPPCC) is an advisory body that sits alongside the NPC. It includes members of China's eight minor "democratic parties," business leaders, intellectuals, ethnic minority representatives, religious figures, and Hong Kong and Macau delegates. The CPPCC has no legislative power. Its primary function is united front work — bringing non-CCP elites into the fold, giving them a sense of participation, and channeling their advice to the party. The CPPCC also serves as a vehicle for the party's overseas influence strategies, as some members have roles connecting mainland China with diaspora communities worldwide.

The key point for Canadian audiences: China's legislature does not debate in the way Western parliaments do. It legitimizes rather than legislates in the independent sense. The real legislative process happens inside party committees, behind closed doors, long before the NPC convenes.

Party vs Government: Why It Matters

The defining structural feature of China's political system is the dual hierarchy of party and state. Every level of government — from the central State Council down to a village committee — has a corresponding party structure that sits above it and controls it. Every government ministry has an internal party committee. Every state-owned enterprise has a party secretary. Every university, every hospital, and every major private company with more than a handful of employees has a party cell embedded within it.

The rule is straightforward: the party secretary outranks the government head at every level. In a province, the Party Secretary outranks the Governor. In a city, the Party Secretary outranks the Mayor. In a ministry, the internal party committee outranks the minister on matters of political direction and personnel. The government official manages administration; the party official sets the political line and controls appointments.

This dual structure has profound practical consequences. It means that when a Chinese official makes a commitment — whether on trade, climate, or consular access — the real question is whether that commitment has party backing at the appropriate level. A promise from a ministry official without party endorsement may not hold. Conversely, once the party makes a decision, the entire state apparatus moves in lockstep to implement it with remarkable speed and coordination.

Under Xi Jinping, the party side of this dual structure has been dramatically strengthened. Xi has created or empowered numerous party-led Leading Small Groups (now often called "commissions") that directly oversee policy areas previously managed by the government. The result is further centralization of power in the party — and specifically in Xi's hands — at the expense of government technocrats and institutional processes.

Why it matters for Canada

When analyzing Chinese policy toward Canada, always ask: was this a party-level decision or a bureaucratic one? The distinction has real consequences. Party-level decisions — like the detention of Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor in December 2018 — are made at the highest levels and are extremely difficult to reverse through normal diplomatic channels. Bureaucratic decisions — like a routine customs inspection delay — may be resolvable through standard government-to-government engagement. Misidentifying which type you are dealing with leads to mismatched responses and wasted effort.

Provincial and Local Governance

China is a vast country with enormous regional variation, and its administrative geography reflects this complexity. The People's Republic is divided into:

  • 23 provinces (including Taiwan, which China claims but does not control)
  • 5 autonomous regions (Tibet, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, Ningxia) — nominally self-governing for ethnic minorities, but in practice tightly controlled by the party
  • 4 directly-administered municipalities (Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, Chongqing) — major cities with provincial-level status
  • 2 Special Administrative Regions (Hong Kong and Macau) — governed under "one country, two systems," though Hong Kong's autonomy has been substantially curtailed since 2020

Provincial and local leaders are appointed by the center, not elected by local populations. The party's Organization Department manages a vast cadre rotation system, regularly moving leaders between provinces and between provincial and central roles. This system ensures loyalty to Beijing and prevents local power bases from becoming too entrenched — though it also means that local leaders sometimes lack deep knowledge of the regions they govern.

Despite centralized appointment, local implementation can diverge significantly from central policy. China has a long history of the dynamic captured in the phrase "the mountains are high and the emperor is far away" (山高皇帝远). Provincial leaders must balance central directives with local economic realities, social pressures, and their own career incentives. A trade policy imposed by Beijing may be enthusiastically enforced in one province and quietly softened in another, depending on local economic dependencies.

For Canadians doing business in China, this means that conditions can vary dramatically from city to city. A business-friendly environment in Shanghai does not guarantee the same reception in a smaller inland city. Regulatory enforcement, judicial reliability, and the political climate for foreign businesses all vary by locality. Understanding which level of government you are actually dealing with — and what latitude that level has — is essential practical knowledge.

The Military: PLA and the CMC

The People's Liberation Army (PLA) is not the national army of China in the way that the Canadian Armed Forces belong to the Canadian state. The PLA is the armed wing of the Chinese Communist Party. It exists to defend the party and its rule, not the nation in the abstract. This distinction is not rhetorical — it is enshrined in China's constitutional order and has profound implications for how military decisions are made.

The PLA is commanded by the Central Military Commission (CMC), which reports directly to the party, not to the State Council or the NPC. The CMC chairman is Xi Jinping — the same person who holds the titles of General Secretary and President. In China's system, control of the military is considered the most critical lever of power, captured in Mao Zedong's famous dictum: "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun" — and therefore "the party commands the gun; the gun must never be allowed to command the party."

The PLA has undergone sweeping modernization and restructuring under Xi. Its current branches are:

  • PLA Army (PLAA) — Ground forces, the historically dominant branch now being slimmed in favour of other services
  • PLA Navy (PLAN) — Rapidly expanding, now the world's largest navy by number of vessels. Central to South China Sea and Taiwan Strait operations
  • PLA Air Force (PLAAF) — Modernizing fast, with stealth fighters and long-range strike capabilities
  • PLA Rocket Force (PLARF) — Controls China's nuclear and conventional missile arsenal
  • PLA Strategic Support Force (PLASSF) — Handles space, cyber, electronic warfare, and information operations

Xi has also waged a massive anti-corruption campaign within the PLA, purging dozens of senior officers. This has simultaneously improved military readiness and ensured personal loyalty to Xi as CMC chairman.

Why it matters for Canada

PLA activities — whether surveillance balloons traversing North American airspace, naval exercises near Taiwan, or cyber operations targeting Canadian networks — reflect party strategic decisions, not independent military adventurism. There is no rogue general problem in China; the party controls the gun. This means military signals are inherently political signals. When the PLA increases pressure in the Taiwan Strait, it is because the party leadership has decided to do so. Canadian defence and intelligence analysts must read PLA actions as expressions of party intent, not as isolated military developments.

Key Institutions for Canada Watchers

For Canadians tracking the Canada-China relationship, certain Chinese institutions appear repeatedly in the news and in policy discussions. Understanding what each one does — and what it does not do — is essential for accurate analysis.

MFA — Ministry of Foreign Affairs (外交部)
China's primary diplomatic interface with the world. The MFA manages embassies, conducts negotiations, and delivers official positions through its regular press conferences. Since roughly 2019, the MFA has adopted an aggressive posture often called "wolf warrior diplomacy," with spokespersons using combative language and social media to push back against foreign criticism. However, the MFA is a messenger, not a policymaker. It communicates positions decided by the party leadership and the Foreign Affairs Commission. The MFA is considered a relatively weak ministry within China's bureaucratic hierarchy.
MOFCOM — Ministry of Commerce (商务部)
Handles trade policy, tariff decisions, foreign investment review, anti-dumping investigations, and trade remedy measures. MOFCOM is the institution Canadians most directly encounter in trade disputes — it was MOFCOM that administered the restrictions on Canadian canola imports in 2019 and manages ongoing trade friction over agricultural products, minerals, and manufactured goods. MOFCOM also oversees China's foreign trade sanctions regime and its "unreliable entities list."
UFWD — United Front Work Department (统战部)
One of the most important party organs for understanding Chinese influence abroad. The UFWD manages the party's relationships with non-CCP groups: ethnic minorities, religious organizations, business elites, overseas Chinese communities, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau. It coordinates influence operations that seek to shape overseas public opinion, co-opt diaspora communities, and cultivate political contacts in foreign countries. The UFWD has been central to Canada's foreign interference debate, with multiple reports identifying united front-linked organizations operating in Canadian cities.
MSS — Ministry of State Security (国安部)
China's primary intelligence and counterintelligence agency, combining functions that in Canada are split between CSIS and the RCMP. The MSS conducts espionage operations abroad, monitors foreign intelligence activities within China, and increasingly conducts cyber operations. It has been implicated in espionage cases involving Canada, including alleged operations targeting Canadian political figures, government employees, and technology companies. The MSS operates with considerable secrecy even within China's own system.
NDRC — National Development and Reform Commission (发改委)
China's powerful economic planning body, sometimes called a "mini State Council" for the breadth of its authority. The NDRC sets industrial policy, manages price controls for key commodities, approves major infrastructure projects, and coordinates China's long-term economic strategy including Five-Year Plans. For Canada, the NDRC matters because its decisions shape China's demand for natural resources, its technology self-sufficiency drive, and the industrial subsidies that affect global competition in sectors where Canadian firms operate.
Taiwan Affairs Office (国台办)
Manages the party and state's policy toward Taiwan. Cross-strait relations have become the single most dangerous flashpoint in Asia-Pacific security. A military conflict over Taiwan would directly affect Canada through its alliance commitments, its economic exposure to the region, and the disruption of global semiconductor supply chains that run through Taiwan. The Taiwan Affairs Office's statements and actions are closely watched as indicators of Beijing's intentions regarding unification and the possible use of force.

Decision-Making: How Policy Happens

One of the most difficult aspects of understanding China for outside observers is the opacity of its decision-making process. In democracies, policy debates happen in legislatures, in the press, and through public consultations. In China, the most consequential decisions are made behind closed doors, within party structures that do not publish minutes, transcripts, or vote tallies.

Nevertheless, China scholars have identified a general pattern for how major policy decisions move through the system:

  1. Internal Party Deliberation: A policy issue is identified and discussed within relevant party bodies. For major issues, this begins at the level of the General Secretary's personal attention and the PSC. For sector-specific issues, it may start in a party department or a Leading Small Group.
  2. Leading Small Groups / Commissions: These are powerful coordination bodies, often chaired by Xi Jinping himself, that bring together party and government officials from multiple agencies. They are where cross-cutting policy is hammered out. Under Xi, groups on foreign affairs, cybersecurity, finance, and "comprehensively deepening reform" have been particularly influential.
  3. Politburo and PSC Discussion: Major decisions are discussed and approved at the Politburo or PSC level. The PSC meets regularly; the full Politburo meets roughly monthly. Decisions made here carry the highest authority.
  4. State Council Implementation: Once the party has set direction, the State Council translates party decisions into implementable government policy — drafting regulations, allocating budgets, and issuing directives to ministries.
  5. Ministry and Local Execution: Individual ministries and provincial governments execute policy on the ground. This is where the rubber meets the road, and where the gap between central intent and local reality can sometimes be wide.

Because this process is opaque, China watchers have developed a specialized toolkit for reading signals. The most important indicators include:

  • State media language: Subtle changes in Xinhua or People's Daily phrasing can signal major policy shifts. The vocabulary used to describe a relationship (e.g., "friendly" vs. "strategic" vs. "hostile") is chosen with extreme precision.
  • Personnel changes: Who gets promoted, demoted, or purged reveals power dynamics and policy priorities. A new ambassador, a reshuffled ministry, or a purged official all carry information.
  • Institutional statements: When the MFA says something, it may be routine. When the PSC or Central Committee issues a statement, it carries the weight of the party's full authority. The level of the institution that speaks tells you how seriously to take the signal.
  • Timing: China's political calendar matters. Decisions made just before a Party Congress carry different weight than those made just after. Policies announced during the Two Sessions have been formally endorsed. Actions taken during politically sensitive anniversaries (June 4, October 1) are loaded with symbolic meaning.

For Canadian policymakers and analysts, learning to read these signals is not optional — it is the primary way to anticipate Chinese behavior before it manifests in actions that affect Canada directly.

Glossary of Key Terms

English Term Chinese Definition
CCP 中国共产党 Communist Party of China, the ruling party since 1949. Also abbreviated CPC in official Chinese English-language usage. Approximately 98 million members.
NPC 全国人民代表大会 National People's Congress, China's legislature. Approximately 3,000 delegates, meets annually in March. Ratifies rather than initiates legislation.
CPPCC 中国人民政治协商会议 Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference. Advisory body with a united front function. Includes non-CCP members but has no legislative power.
State Council 国务院 China's cabinet, led by the Premier. The executive arm of government responsible for implementing party decisions through ministries and commissions.
Politburo Standing Committee 中央政治局常务委员会 The top 7 leaders of the CCP and China's supreme decision-making body. Members are ranked in protocol order, with the General Secretary at number one.
CMC 中央军事委员会 Central Military Commission, the body that commands the PLA. Chaired by Xi Jinping. Reports to the party, not the state.
Two Sessions 两会 The annual concurrent meetings of the NPC and CPPCC, held in March in Beijing. China's most important annual political event, where government work reports are delivered and policy direction is formally endorsed.
Leading Small Group 领导小组 Informal but powerful policy coordination bodies that bring together officials from multiple party and government agencies. Under Xi, many have been upgraded to "commissions" with greater formal authority.
United Front 统一战线 A party strategy to co-opt non-party groups both domestically and abroad. Managed by the United Front Work Department. Relevant to Canada's foreign interference debate.
Wolf Warrior Diplomacy 战狼外交 The aggressive, combative diplomatic style adopted by Chinese officials and MFA spokespersons since approximately 2019. Named after a popular Chinese action film. Marked by confrontational social media use and sharp public rhetoric.
